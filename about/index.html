Ekaterina Shalel

How a medical student and founder ended up studying how AI systems understand, evaluate and select companies and the people behind them.

Also published as Katya Shalel, Екатерина Шалель, Кети Шалель

I opened my first business while I was still in medical school at Sechenov University in Moscow. Four online stores, meant to cover the bills until I graduated. They outgrew that, and a few years later I sold them.

Then I did what I had trained for and went into cosmetology, close enough to the products to know what actually differs between them and what is packaging. Then I went into technology, which looked like a clean break at the time and turned out not to be one.

While building AI products, I became interested in a problem that reach alone could not solve: an AI system can recognize a company and still reconstruct it poorly, lack independent evidence for important claims, or leave it out when a user asks whom to choose.

I started measuring that problem directly: what models retrieve, how they describe an entity, which sources support the answer, what changes after an intervention, and where the result remains unstable. That became my independent AI legibility practice.

Recognition is not selection. That gap is the work.

Today I work on AI legibility: whether a system can retrieve the right entity, understand it without guessing, find independent corroboration for important claims, and select it under specified decision conditions. The method is measure, diagnose, intervene and verify. Selection is observed, not guaranteed.

In 2026 I founded getmai.ai, an AI engineering company for businesses that run on appointments and repeat visits: clinics, dental practices, salons, spas, wellness groups, beauty retail. It is a separate company with its own team and its own site, and it is the operating side of the same interest. Legibility is about being read correctly. Getmai is about what happens after someone decides to book.

The through line is choice. AI increasingly sits between a company and the person deciding whom to trust, hire or buy from. Knowing that an entity exists is only the first step; the harder question is what evidence the system can defend when it has to compare alternatives and choose.

Medicine left me with a habit that matters here: separate observation from inference and do not claim more than the evidence supports. A model rationale is not proof of its internal mechanism, and a before-and-after change is not automatically causal.
